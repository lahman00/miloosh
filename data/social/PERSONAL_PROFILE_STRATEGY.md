## Whether it can responsibly contribute to Miloosh distribution

Yes, with real constraints. A founder with zero posting history has more credibility upside than downside from starting to post — but only if the content reads as genuine perspective, not a feed of reposted company links. The company page and the personal profile need to stay visibly different: the company page states facts about products; the founder profile can state opinions about the *process* of building Miloosh and about software-buying as a discipline — a register the company page shouldn't use.

## Proposed cadence

~2 high-quality posts/week. Not more — a founder profile that posts daily reads as a content mill, and the company page's own cadence (3×/week) is already the primary channel. The personal profile's job is depth and perspective, not frequency.

## 4 draft candidates — NOT published, for review

**1 — On why Miloosh exists (founder's-eye view, not the company's)**
> Building a software comparison site made me realize how much of "software research" online is just SEO content wearing a research costume.
>
> Most "X vs Y" articles are written by someone who's used neither X nor Y, generated to rank, updated never. The pricing table is from 2023. The "winner" is whichever one has the better affiliate payout.
>
> I didn't start Miloosh to fix all of that. I started it because I got tired of not trusting any of it.

**2 — A real observation from building the comparison dataset**
> Something I didn't expect going in: the hardest part of building software comparisons isn't gathering the data. It's the products that change their pricing model mid-quarter and don't announce it anywhere public.
>
> "Verified as of a date" turned out to be a much more honest promise than "accurate," because accurate implies a permanence most SaaS pricing pages don't actually have.

**3 — A genuine opinion on buyer behavior (no product name-dropping)**
> Most software-buying mistakes I've seen aren't about picking the wrong tool. They're about picking a tool before anyone wrote down what "wrong" would even look like for that team.
>
> A shortlist of two clear requirements beats an unstructured trial of six tools almost every time.

**4 — Process transparency (building in public, honestly)**
> Two weeks into Miloosh, the thing I'm most careful about isn't the product — it's not letting "we need traffic" quietly turn into "let's fudge a comparison to make it more shareable."
>
> The rule I set for myself: if a claim can't be traced to something the vendor themselves published, it doesn't go on the site. Slower. Also the only way I'd trust it if I were the reader.
